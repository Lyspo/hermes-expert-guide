Unattended means nobody is watching, which includes nobody watching the meter. This
lesson is the arithmetic, and it starts from a figure that reliably shocks people.

## The numbers people actually report

Four figures from published first-hand accounts. None is a benchmark; all are real usage:

| Reported | Context |
|---|---|
| 4 million tokens in two hours | An enthusiastic first session |
| 21,000 tokens for a simple query | Through a messaging gateway |
| 37M input / 120K output, 600 requests | One reader's **day two** |
| Under $20/month | A VPS deployment on a cheap model |

The spread between the last two is the entire lesson. The same software costs 37 million
tokens a day or twenty dollars a month depending on decisions this lesson describes.

## Why the gateway costs more

Reported gateway overhead runs **two to three times CLI cost for the same question**,
and the mechanism is not mysterious: the gateway re-sends platform state, user state,
conversation history, skills, memory and the system prompt every turn.

Which is to say — it re-sends the diagram from `05/01`, every message. In the terminal
you feel like you are continuing a conversation. Through Telegram you are, from the
model's perspective, arriving fresh each time with a lot of luggage.

## The fixed floor

Before your message, every call carries the assembled prefix. The project's own tracker
puts it at **73% of each API call — around 13.9 thousand tokens.**

That is the floor. A one-word question and a paragraph of analysis cost almost the same
in input tokens, which inverts the intuition that short questions are cheap.

The lever that does most for this is caching. Prompt caching is auto-enabled for Claude
models on native Anthropic, OpenRouter and Nous Portal, with a configurable five-minute
or one-hour TTL and breakpoints on the system prompt and skill blocks. A cached prefix
is roughly ten times cheaper to read than to write — so a long-lived session is
dramatically cheaper per turn than a series of fresh ones.

**Consequence worth acting on:** unattended work made of many short fresh sessions —
one cron job per minute, say — pays full price for that prefix every time. Fewer, longer
runs are cheaper than more, shorter ones, which is the opposite of how people usually
schedule.

## Five settings that change the number

**1 — A cheaper model.** The largest single lever, and the least clever. Published
deployments running under twenty dollars a month are running mid-tier models, not
frontier ones.

**2 — Auxiliary models.** Hermes can route background work off your main model
entirely — compression, the background review, the curator, triage — each with its own
provider and model slot:

```yaml
auxiliary:
  compression:
    model: ""          # empty = main model
  background_review:
    provider: openrouter
    model: google/gemini-3-flash-preview
```

Remember the trade from `06/05`: a review routed to a different model replays a
24-message digest rather than the full conversation, so you are paying in fidelity.

**3 — Fewer toolsets.** Every enabled toolset's schemas ship on every call forever.
`agent.disabled_toolsets` is a cost control as much as a security one, and for an
unattended job the right toolset list is usually much shorter than your interactive one.

**4 — Guardrails.** For anything unattended, cap the loop:

```yaml
agent:
  max_turns: 90
```

At the limit the agent gets one grace call to wrap up. There are also
`tool_loop_guardrails`, and the documentation recommends `hard_stop_enabled: true`
specifically for gateway, cron and Kanban workers — which is to say, exactly the contexts
this module is about.

**5 — Skip the model entirely.** `--no-agent` cron scripts and `deliver_only` webhook
routes cost nothing per run. A surprising share of scheduled work is a check or a
notification rather than a judgement.

<Depth min="operator">

The tiered-routing pattern several practitioners report is worth copying: a cheap fast
model for mechanical work, a mid-tier one for anything delicate, and the expensive model
reserved for the cases that actually need it. One published account puts the saving at
around forty dollars and ten hours.

A different lever, also reported: rewriting verbose tool output before it enters context
cut 60–90% of context tokens in one integration. Terminal output is frequently the
largest single contributor and `tool_output.max_bytes` is the blunt version of the same
idea.

</Depth>

## Measuring instead of guessing

<Command verified="v0.19.0">hermes insights --days 7</Command>

Token, cost and activity analytics, filterable by source with `--source`, so you can
compare what the CLI costs against what the gateway costs on your own usage rather than
on this lesson's figures.

The dashboard's Analytics tab shows the same over 7, 30 and 90 days — including
**cache-hit percentage**, which is the number to watch. A low cache-hit rate on a
long-running deployment means something is invalidating the prefix, and that is a bug
worth finding rather than a bill worth paying.

## What to take from this

The floor is around 13.9K tokens per call, the gateway multiplies your per-message cost
by two to three, and fresh short sessions pay that floor repeatedly.

So: pick a cheaper model, route background work to auxiliary slots, cut toolsets you do
not need, cap the loop, and use the no-model paths where the work is not a judgement.
Then run `hermes insights` and check whether any of it worked.
