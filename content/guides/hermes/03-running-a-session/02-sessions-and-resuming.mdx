Closing the terminal does not end anything. Every session is a row in a SQLite database
at `~/.hermes/state.db`, with its full message history, and it is still there tomorrow.

That single fact changes how you use the tool. You do not need to finish a task in one
sitting, and you do not need to re-explain context you already gave.

## Getting back

The short form covers most cases:

<Command verified="v0.19.0">hermes --continue</Command>

`-c` is the same thing. It resumes the most recent session.

When "most recent" is not what you want, there are three other handles:

```bash
hermes -c "my project"                     # by name — latest in that lineage
hermes --resume 20260225_143052_a1b2c3     # by session id
hermes --resume "refactoring auth"         # by title
```

Resuming by title is the one worth building a habit around, because it works only if
you name things. `/title My Session Name` inside a session, or `hermes sessions rename`
from outside.

<MarginNote>
A session id encodes its own timestamp — `20260225_143052` is a date and a time. Useful
when you know roughly when you did something but not what you called it.
</MarginNote>

## Lineage, and why "latest in that lineage" is not pedantry

When compression fires, the session does not continue in place. A new session is created
with the summarised history, and it records the old one as its parent.

So a long conversation that has compressed three times is four rows, chained. Lineage
tracking is what makes that chain navigable — resuming "my project" gets you the newest
link, not the original.

This matters when you go looking for something you remember saying. The message is in
the lineage, but it may not be in the session you land in. That is what `session_search`
is for, and it is covered in `05/04`.

## What the CLI gives you

```bash
hermes sessions list
hermes sessions rename <id> <title>
```

The full subcommand set goes further — `browse`, `export`, `delete`, `prune`, `archive`
and `stats` — which is more of a data-management surface than people expect from a chat
tool, and appropriate for something accumulating every conversation you have.

## Background sessions

Different thing, same neighbourhood. `/background <prompt>` starts a *separate* agent
session in a daemon thread:

<Transcript fidelity="reconstructed" source="CLI documentation, v0.19.0">
{`🔄 Background task #1 started: "..."
Task ID: bg_143022_a1b2c3`}
</Transcript>

It inherits your model, provider, toolsets and reasoning settings, runs without blocking
you, and surfaces its result as a panel when it finishes. You can have several going at
once.

The property that defines it: **it is isolated from your foreground history and does not
appear in it.** The background agent cannot see what you are doing and you will not find
its work in your conversation. It is a second worker, not a delegated subtask — and the
distinction from `delegate_task` is real, since that one reports back into the
conversation that spawned it.

Use it for the thing you want done while you keep thinking. Do not use it for anything
whose output needs to inform the conversation you are in.

<Depth min="operator">

`Ctrl+Z` suspends the whole session to the background on Unix, which is a different
mechanism again — shell job control rather than a Hermes feature. It is the fastest way
to get your terminal back without losing state, and `fg` returns you.

</Depth>

## What to take from this

Sessions are durable objects, not scrollback. Resume with `-c`, name them so you can
resume by title later, and remember that compression forks a session rather than editing
it — so "the conversation" is often a chain rather than a row.

Background sessions are a separate worker with a separate history. Useful, and not the
same as delegation.
